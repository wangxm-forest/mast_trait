\section{Method} 
\subsection{Data Collection}
\subsubsection{Trait selection}
We selected traits based on three hypotheses proposed to explain the evolution of masting. For the predator satiation hypothesis, we selected traits associated with seed attractiveness to predators and the potential for surviving in seed caches, including seed weight, fruit size, oil content, dispersal mode and seed dormancy. For the pollination coupling hypothesis, we aimed for traits related to higher pollination efficiency: pollination mode and reproductive type. For resource matching hypothesis, we included traits responsible for higher flexibility in resource use under varying environmental conditions.
\subsubsection{Data scraping}
For our analyses, we obtained the full species list from the two volumes of \textit{Silvics of North America} \cite{burns1990silvics,burns1990silvics2} (hereafter referred as \textit{Silvics}). We extracted masting data primarily from \textit{Silvics}, using MASTREE as a supplementary source when there was no description on the seed production patterns in \textit{Silvics}. Pollination mode and seed dispersal data were also obtained mainly from \textit{Silvics}. We obtained seed weight data from the Seed Information Database by the Society for Ecological Restoration; gathered seed dormancy data from \textit{Seeds} by Baskin and Baskin and accessed the leaf longevity and oil content data through the TRY database. We compiled fruit size and drought tolerance data from multiple sources, including: \textit{Silvics}, the USDA Fire Effects Information System, the USA National Phenology Network, and The Gymnosperm Database. %emwSep21 -- all your online citations need to be URL or such citations with a date accessed etc.. We defined masting based on qualitative descriptions of seed production patterns. Although recent studies emphasize quantitative definitions of masting, many forestry manuals view masting as a species-level categorical trait, including \textit{Silvics}. Thus, we classified species according to key phrases describing their seed production patterns. Species described as producing "a good crop almost every year" were classified as weak masting species, whereas species described as producing "a good crop every several years" or having "irregular seed production" were classified as strong masting species.

For some statistical analyses we grouped or otherwise adjusted the data on traits related to masting hypotheses. For numerical traits with a range (seed weight and fruit size), we recorded the full range from the source, and took the mean value for further analyses. For categorical traits (pollination mode, reproductive type and dispersal mode), we grouped them into broader categories, For example, we classified pollination mode into wind, animal (bird, bat, or insect), or both. The complete raw and processed datasets are available through KNB.

We then constructed an ultrametric phylogenetic tree in R using the \texttt{phytools} (v. 2.4-4) and \texttt{ape} (v. 5.7-1) packages. We calculated the half-life using the equation $(\log(2)/\alpha)$ and compared it with the tree height (standardized to 1). This metric represents the time required for the influence of ancestral states to decrease by half.

We evaluated both two- and three-dimendional solutions \(k = 2, 3\) and selected \(k = 3\) based on the lower stress score. fix_unicode <- function(x) {
  x <- gsub("\u2212", "-", x)
  x <- gsub("–", "-", x)
  x <- gsub("—", "-", x)
  x <- gsub("%", "\\\\%", x)
  x <- gsub("\\balpha\\b", "$\\\\alpha$", x)
  x <- gsub("\\blambda\\b", "$\\\\lambda$", x)
  x
}
