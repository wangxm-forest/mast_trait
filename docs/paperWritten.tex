\section{Introduction}
In 2025, Japan experienced the highest number of bear attacks on historical record. A recent research has linked this issue to food deficit because of the disruptions in periodic forest food supply which is usually manifested as masting \citep{xiao2026compound}. Masting is the phenomenon of synchronized production of seeds or other reproductive structures among individuals within a population \citep{kelly1994evolutionary,kelly2002mast, pearse2016mechanisms}. Masting has important ecological consequences and has long been studied in the context of seed predation\citep{janzen1971seed, kelly1994evolutionary}. By producing large seed crops in mast years, plants can overwhelm seed predators, increasing the proportion of seeds that escape predation and potentially enhancing seedling recruitment. Earlier in the reproductive cycle, synchronized flowering may improve pollination efficiency. Throughout the reproductive cycle, reproduction requires substantial resource investment, the resource availability for reproduction may determine the success of a mast event. Together, masting may be shaped by selective pressures and constraints acting at different stages of the reproductive cycle.

Based on interactions happened during different reproductive stages, multiple hypotheses exist to explain the evolution of masting, including pollination coupling, predator satiation and resource budgeting. Our understanding of masting has advanced considerably with more data collected and looking at the masting at a finer scale. People have discovered that wind pollination is more prevalent among strongly masting species, consistent with the hypothesis that synchronized flowering enhances pollination efficiency for wind pollinated species. A finer-scale investigations of the relationship between seed dispersers and masting have found that masting is weaker when the benefits of synchronized reproduction in satiating seed predators are reduced \citep{qiu2023masting}, stating the satiation of seed predator might be an important driver for masting to work. More recent studies have also explored resource-based explanations, using leaf and shoot traits to investigate how species differ in their capacity to respond to resource fluctuations \citep{journe2023evolution}. Environmental cues can synchronize reproductive activity among individuals within a population, but they cannot, on their own, explain why species differ in the strength of their masting patterns. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and their relative importance may vary among species according to their life-history strategies.

It is then important to connect traits and hypotheses and potential evolutionary drivers when considering what shapes the masting behaviour for certain species. Functional traits provide a potential strength of connecting theoretical hypotheses of masting to measurable differences among speceis. Traits associated with different hypotheses may help evaluate whether masting overall may benefit the species. Masting only make sense when there is a density-dependent advantage because their is also costs of masting such as the risk of delayed reproduction or the possibility of missing a good recruitment year. By examining these traits in relation to masting behavior, it may be possible to evaluate the relative support for different hypotheses and identify the characteristics associated with variation in reproductive synchrony.


In this study, we investigated the relationships between traits and masting behaviour to evaluate the potential mechanisms underlying masting. Specifically, we tested whether traits associated with different hypotheses explain differences in masting behaviours among species and whether these relationships are similar among closely related species. Here we use North American tree data to test this framework since ecologically and evolutionary they are more closely related.


\section{Method} 
\subsection{Data Collection}
\subsubsection{Trait selection}
We selected traits based on three hypotheses proposed to explain the evolution of masting. For the predator satiation hypothesis, we selected traits associated with seed attractiveness to predators and the potential for surviving in seed caches, including seed weight, fruit size, oil content, dispersal mode and seed dormancy. For the pollination coupling hypothesis, we aimed for traits related to higher pollination efficiency: pollination mode and reproductive type. For resource matching hypothesis, we included traits responsible for higher flexibility in resource use under varying environmental conditions.
\subsubsection{Data scraping}
For our analyses, we obtained the full species list from the two volumes of \textit{Silvics of North America} \citep{burns1990silvics,burns1990silvics2} (hereafter referred as \textit{Silvics}). We extracted masting data primarily from \textit{Silvics}, using MASTREE as a supplementary source when there was no description on the seed production patterns in \textit{Silvics}. Pollination mode and seed dispersal data were also obtained mainly from \textit{Silvics}. We obtained seed weight data from the Seed Information Database by the Society for Ecological Restoration \citep{sid}; gathered seed dormancy data from \textit{Seeds} by Baskin and Baskin and accessed the leaf longevity and oil content data through the TRY database. We compiled fruit size and drought tolerance data from multiple sources, including: \textit{Silvics}, the USDA Fire Effects Information System \citep{USDAfire}, the USA National Phenology Network \citep{phenology}, and The Gymnosperm Database \citep{gymno}.

We defined masting based on qualitative descriptions of seed production patterns. Although recent studies emphasize quantitative definitions of masting, many forestry manuals view masting as a species-level categorical trait, including \textit{Silvics}. Thus, we classified species according to key phrases describing their seed production patterns. Species described as producing "a good crop almost every year" were classified as weak masting species, whereas species described as producing "a good crop every several years" or having "irregular seed production" were classified as strong masting species.

For some statistical analyses we grouped or otherwise adjusted the data on traits related to masting hypotheses. For numerical traits with a range (seed weight and fruit size), we recorded the full range from the source, and took the mean value for further analyses. (We took the log form of seed weight and fruit size because the range of data is large.) For categorical traits (pollination mode, reproductive type and dispersal mode), we grouped them into broader categories, For example, we classified pollination mode into wind, animal (birds, bats, or insects), or both. The complete raw and processed datasets are available through KNB.

To analyze the relationship between masting pattern and individual traits, we used logistic regression, treating masting as a binary response variable. Given our species-level analyses we used a phylogenetic logistic regression approach (\texttt{phyloglm} function in the \texttt{phylolm} package; v. 2.6.2). To construct the phylogenetic tree displaying the phylogenetic structure of our species list, we verified all species names using the  \texttt{WorldFlora} package (v. 1.14-5) and updated all species names according to WCVP Backbone data (2025). We then constructed an ultrametric phylogenetic tree in R using the \texttt{phytools} (v. 2.4-4) and \texttt{ape} (v. 5.7-1) packages. To test the phylogenetic correction for each trait, we calculated the half-life using the equation $(\log(2)/\alpha)$ and compared it with the tree height (standardized to 1). This metric represents the time required for the influence of ancestral states to decrease by half. For more inerpretation, see \cite{cooper2016cautionary}
\subsubsection{Masting pattern with a multivariate analysis}
In addition to these univariate analyses looking at the relationship between masting and each individual trait, we also conducted a multivariate analysis to test whether traits related to any single hypothesis is driving the masting pattern, using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS, implemented using \texttt{metaMDS} function in the \texttt{vegan} v. 2.7-1 package). We evaluated both two- and three-dimendional solutions \(k = 2, 3\) and selected \(k = 3\) based on the lower stress score. To check for potential patterns, we plotted trait distributions on two panels (NMDS1 vs.\ NMDS2 and NMDS2 vs.\ NMDS3).
\subsubsection{Phylogenetic signal for individual traits}
To check for the phylogenetic signal for every single traits as well as masting, we used different metrics depending on the type of data. For traits that were binary, we calculated Fritz and Purvis' D-statistics using the function \texttt{phylo.d} in the \texttt{caper}  (v. 1.0.4) package. For categorical traits, we estimated the Pagel's $\lambda$ using the function \texttt{fitDiscrete} in the \texttt{geiger} (v. 2.0.11) package. For continuous traits, we calculated Pagel's $\lambda$ using the function \texttt{phylosig} in the \texttt{phytools} (v. 2.4-4) package.

\section{Results}
\subsubsection{Structure of Data}
In total, our dataset included 188 species, comprising 124 angiosperm species and 64 gymnosperm species. Among angiosperms, 79\% (98/124) of species had masting data available, with strong masting and weak masting species occurring in approximately equal proportions 52\% (51/98). Among gymnosperms, 95\% (61/64) of species had masting data available, and nearly all were classified as strong masting species 89\% (54/61).

We were not able to find complete trait data for every single trait for all species with masting data. The traits with the most available data was seed weight. The traits with the sparse data were oil content and leaf longevity (Fig. \ref{fig:rawangio} and Fig. \ref{fig:rawcon}).
\subsubsection{Results for Each Hypothesis}
We found relationships between traits related to predator satiation and pollination efficiency and strong masting for angiosperm species. Among the traits related to predator satiation, seed weight was the only traits significantly related to masting (p-value = 0.02, Table \ref{tab:regressionangio}). Larger seed weight was associated with a greater probability of strong masting (Figure \ref{fig:rawangio}). For angiosperms only, increasing seed weight threefold (from a median seed weight of 90.9g to 272.7g) increased the predicted likelihood of strong masting from 0.44 to 0.5 (Figure \ref{fig:seedweight}). The effect was stronger among species with biotically dispersed seeds, where increasing seed weight threefold (from a median seed weight of 1087.15mg to 3261.45mg) increased the predicted likelihood of strong masting from 0.73 to 0.81 (Table \ref{tab:regressionseed}; Figure \ref{fig:seedweight}). 

Among traits related to pollination efficiency, pollination mode was the only pollination-related trait significantly associated with strong masting (p-value = 0.02, Table \ref{tab:regressionangio}) for angiosperm. Wind-pollinated species had a predicted likelihood of strong masting of 0.59, compared with 0.2 for animal-pollinated species. All gymnosperm species in our dataset were wind-pollinated, and gymnosperms contained a much higher proportion of strong masting species overall. This can also indicate that wind pollination is associated with strong masting.
\subsubsection{Multivariate analysis}
Strong and weak masting species were not clearly separated along any axis in the NMDS analysis (Fig. \ref{fig:nmds}). We found no evidence that combinations of traits associated with any of the three hypotheses explained variation in masting pattern.
\subsubsection{Phylogenetic signal}
Masting pattern showed a moderate phylogenetic signal in both gymnosperms (D = 0.51) and angiosperms (D = 0.58) (Table \ref{tab:dstat}).

Seed weight has strong phylogenetic signal for both angiosperm ($\lambda$ = 0.96) and gymnosperm ($\lambda$ = 0.98). This pattern is also evident in Fig. \ref{fig:coneptangio}. Dispersal mode, leaf longevity, pollination mode and reproductive type also showed high phylogenetic signal in Table \ref{tab:lambda}. However, these values should be interpreted cautiously because Pagel's $\lambda$ is estimated differently for categorical and continuous traits, making direct comparisons inappropriate.

\section{Discussion}
We found that masting was associated with traits related to both pollination and seed dispersal, providing support for multiple mechanisms underlying interannual variation in seed production. The positive relationship between wind pollination and strong masting in angiosperm supports the pollination coupling, while the positive relationship between seed weight and strong masting provides evidence consistent with the predator satiation. These associations point to selective pressure acting at both early and late stages of the reproductive cycle, which are critical for successful reproduction and gene transmission.

Previous literature has proposed a positive relationship between seed mass and masting \citep{kelly1994evolutionary}, with one explanation being that larger seeds require more resource investment and simply cannot reproduce every year. In contrast, the other study has reported  opposite relationship between seed mass and masting, suggesting that small seeds are usually associated with seed bank which reduce the fitness costs associated with years of low or failed reproduction \citep{journe2023evolution}. We found a weak but significant positive relationship between seed mass and masting, suggesting that the association between these traits may reflect processes beyond the resource costs of producing large seeds. For predator satiation to favour synchronized reproduction, seed crops must provide a sufficiently large resource to overwhelm seed consumers \citep{janzen1971seed,kelly1994evolutionary}. In some species, seed predators also function as seed dispersers. Larger seeds may provide greater rewards to animal dispersers and therefore facilitate dispersal of these species. At the same time, larger seeds generally contain greater nutritional reserves, which can increase the probability of successful germination and establishment following dispersal, even in the case when seeds experience some degree of predation or damage \citep{bonal2007satiation, moles2004seedling}. Thus, large seeds may provide benefits at multiple stages following seed production, from attracting dispersers to supporting seedling establishment. Seed size and seed crop size are also often subject to a trade-off because greater investment in individual seeds can reduce the number of seeds\citep{castro2024seed, parciak2002seed}. Masting may partially relieve this constraint by concentrating reproduction into episodic years, allowing a species to produce a large number of relatively costly seeds while at the same time reducing the proportion of seeds consumed. Together, these processes could generate selection favouring reproductive strategies that enhance seed dispersal and survival.


\subsection{Scale dependence in trait–masting relationships}
Recent work has emphasized that masting is better represented as a continuous rather than categorical trait, which is mostly available at the population level \citep{bogdziewicz2024evolutionary, bogdziewicz2025dynamics}. The coefficient of variation (CV) is one of the most widely used continuous measures of masting. However, the use of CV as a measure of masting has been widely discussed, as it does not capture all characteristics of masting and can be strongly distorted by years with zero seed production \citep{bogdziewicz2023measure}. At the same time, evaluating the evolutionary associations between traits and masting requires comparisons across species. If a trait has a sufficiently strong and consistent association with the evolution of masting, we expect this relationship to be detectable at the species level using binary masting data.

At the same time, many of the traits considered in our analysis are phenotypically plastic and may vary among populations, potentially in response to local environmental conditions. Thus, traits for which we fail to detect a species -level association with masting may still be important at the population level. There are certain species only exhibit masting under certain environmental conditions\citep{fleurot2023oak, bogdziewicz2025dynamics}. As a result, understanding how reproductive traits are associated with masting may require measurements of both traits and reproductive variation at the population level, considering local environmental conditions and intraspecific variation.

\subsection{Distinguishing selection across types of masting}
Masting has originally been studied primarily as interannual variation in seed production, but recent work has refined the definition between seed-maturation masting and flower masting. These two forms of reproductive synchrony occur at different stages of the reproductive cycle: seed masting focuses on the final reproductive outcome, whereas flower masting occurs at the beginning of reproduction. Importantly, variation in flowering and seed production does not necessarily occur at the same time, suggesting that these two forms of masting may be shapred by different selective pressures. For example, we would expect mechanisms related to pollination to perform stronger selection on flowering synchrony, whereas mechanisms related to seed predation, dispersal and establishment may be more directly associated with synchrony in seed production. Separating species according to their dominant form of masting could there fore provide a more refined test of hypotheses underlying reproductive synchrony. Examining the associations between different types of masting and corresponding subset of reproductive traits therefore is necessary for us to understand how different selective pressures contribute to the evolution of reproductive synchrony at different stages of the reproductive cycle.

\subsection{Phylogenetic signal limits inference}
We found strong phylogenetic signal for many traits we selected and also masting has a phylogenetic signal. For the analysis we did, it is hard to tease out effect of phylogeny from the correlation between single trait and masting. Also, many traits we included have relatively sparse data, and these data are nested in certain clade. This makes the phylogenetic bias a potentially bigger problem.

\subsection{Deep evolutionary split between angiosperm and gymnosperm}
It is also important to look for the evidence of evolution separately for angiosperm and gymnosperm. Gymnosperms and angiosperms occupy fundamentally different functional spaces. In our dataset, the proportion of species exhibit strong masting is greater in gymnosperm, wind pollination is also universal among gymnosperms. The leaf longevity is also in general longer than angiosperm species. The mechanisms behind masting for angiosperm and gymnosperm might be different. The abundant of certain traits and masting in gymnosperm might indicate this is a more important and efficient reproductive strategy for them, but not necessarily for more diverse angiosperm. There might be interests to test if masting was gradually diluted in angiosperm as they transitioned to biotic pollination and faster life histories.

\subsection{Importance of testing within clade}
Given that the evolutionary mechanisms underlying masting may differ among lineages and operate at different biological scales, investigating evolutionary patterns within clades is an important next step. Previous studies have highlighted the potential of clade-specific comparisons to investigate the evolution of masting\citep{satake2021studying}. Clades that contain species exhibiting both strong masting and weak masting such as \textit{Quercus} and \textit{Pinus}, offer particularly valuable opportunities to investigate how traits and selective pressures are associated with variation in masting. To achieve this, we need more comprehensive data on both species-level and population-level for comparison. Such an approach would help our understanding of masting evolution by filling the gaps on how masting varies among populations in response to contemporary ecological conditions and how reproductive strategies have diverged over longer evolutionary timescales. Integrating all these perspectives together will provide a more comprehensive and comparable understanding of how ecological conditions, life-history traits and evolutionary history jointly shape the diversity of reproductive strategies.
